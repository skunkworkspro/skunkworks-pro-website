The premise

A beautiful lie is still a prison.

Genesis begins as an intimate house mystery and widens into a fight over memory itself. In this future, power no longer needs to arrive as brute force. It arrives as care. As relief. As mercy.

That is what makes it terrifying. May wants the truth. Kit remembers too much. The city wants both of them easier to manage.

The system does not erase people. It improves them until nobody can find the original.

Cyberpunk gives Genesis its surveillance, scale, and class violence. Romantasy gives it the ache: two people trying to stay real when every institution around them has learned how to edit pain.

The world

Two cities. One machinery of mercy.

The palette stays closer to Dune — black, sand, amber, rust — but the behaviour is cyberpunk: hard cuts, surveillance lines, warning labels, and a city that feels alive around the reader.

Love after the system

May + Kit

They are not safe for each other. That is why they matter.

May is composed, intelligent, and trained by inheritance to survive beautifully. Kit is restrained, observant, and no longer willing to remain useful only at the edges of someone else’s life.

Their romance is not escape from the plot. It is the test case. If memory can be edited and grief can be softened, then being fully seen becomes the most dangerous intimacy left.

May

Guarded heiress. Emotional armour. A woman being forced out of every inherited script that once kept her safe.

Kit

Quiet danger. Uncomfortable memory. The person who knows what the polished world had to bury to stay beautiful.

The romance

Recognition under pressure: desire, class fracture, unfinished history, and the refusal to become unreal for comfort.
